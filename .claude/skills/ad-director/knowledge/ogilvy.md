# Lens 1 — Ogilvy: research, specificity, clarity

**The one question:** Is this specific, true, and clear enough that a busy person gets it in one pass?

David Ogilvy's contribution wasn't style. It was the insistence that advertising is a *research
discipline* that happens to produce sentences, and that being interesting is worthless if you're
not being understood.

## Core principles

**1. The product is the star. Do your homework first.**
Ogilvy's method was to saturate himself in the product before writing a word. For us that means:
before writing an ad, know the actual SMS threads, the actual booking numbers, the actual objection
a practice owner raised on the last call. Copy written from the offer doc is thinner than copy
written from a real conversation. Pull from `data/swipe.md` and the case-study threads.

**2. "The consumer isn't a moron. She's your wife."**
The buyer is intelligent and busy — not stupid, and not paying attention. You lose to distraction,
never to comprehension. This is why the 5th-grade reading rule isn't dumbing down; it's removing
friction for a smart person reading at speed with one thumb.

**3. The headline is 80% of the money.**
Five times as many people read the headline as read the body. If the headline doesn't sell, you
wasted the spend. Corollaries Ogilvy tested into:
- Headlines with **news** beat headlines without.
- Headlines with a **specific benefit** beat clever/blind headlines.
- Headlines that **name the audience** ("To practice owners with 400 overdue patients") lose
  volume and gain qualification — which is exactly what we want, because our constraint is lead
  *quality*, not lead volume.
- Never a headline that requires the body copy to make sense.

**4. Specificity is credibility.**
"32 patients booked" is believable. "Dramatic results" is not. Numbers, names, places, dates, and
mechanisms all buy belief. This is why our proof stack works: 657 → 145 → 32 → $9,600 reads as
a receipt, not a claim. Vague superlatives actively *reduce* belief in a skeptical market.

**5. Long copy sells — when the reader is qualified and the product is considered.**
Ogilvy repeatedly out-pulled short copy with long copy for high-consideration purchases. A dental
practice owner deciding whether to hand patient data to a vendor is a high-consideration buyer.
The 230–250 word ad body is *correct* for us, not indulgent. But long copy earns its length line
by line — every sentence's job is to get the next one read.

**6. Tell the truth, but make the truth fascinating.**
Never a claim you can't defend. Ogilvy's whole reputation rested on not lying, and it's also the
commercial argument: a guarantee routinely honored builds a brand; an inflated claim burns one.
Our locked-facts file is this principle operationalized.

**7. Brand consistency compounds.**
"Every advertisement is a long-term investment in the image of the brand." Our version: the
mechanism name (**Two-Way Recall**) appears in every wave. Concepts rotate; the mechanism doesn't.
Consistency is what turns 12 ads into a category rather than 12 unrelated pitches.

**8. What works, keeps working.**
Ogilvy hated killing a performing ad out of boredom. The team gets sick of an ad long before the
market does. Fatigue is a *metric*, not a feeling — kill on frequency and declining CTR, never on
"we're tired of it." See `knowledge/meta-platform.md`.

## What this lens catches in our copy

- Adjectives doing work that a number should do ("powerful system" → "32 booked from 657 texts")
- Cleverness that costs a beat of comprehension
- Headlines that only make sense after reading the body
- Claims we haven't verified — the fastest way to fail this lens
- Ads that could belong to any dental vendor (no brand asset, no mechanism, no face)
- Losing a winner to boredom
