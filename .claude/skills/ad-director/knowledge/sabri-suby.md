# Lens 4 — Suby: structure, beats, and earning the click

**The one question:** Does the copy move through the beats in order, and does it earn the click
instead of asking for it?

Sabri Suby's contribution is **structural discipline**. Where Ogilvy governs the sentence and
Schwartz governs the strategy, this lens governs the *sequence* — the order the beats fire in, and
the refusal to ask for action before the reader has been moved.

## The 13-beat structure (locked for Dentiflow, 230–250 words)

This is the shape every Dentiflow long-form Meta ad follows. Range 231–248 words.

1. **Callout + gate** — first five words. Who this is for, and who it isn't.
2. **Container line** — the one-sentence frame of what this is about.
3. **Agitation** — the cost of the status quo, in a picture, not an abstraction.
4. **Mechanism** — Two-Way Recall. Why everything they tried failed.
5–7. **Three ✅ bullets** — led by the ~$9,600 production figure.
8. **Anti-disqualification** — remove the reason they've already invented to opt out
   ("this isn't a new-patient ad campaign," "you don't need to switch software").
9. **Objection pre-handle** — the biggest unspoken one, named before they think it.
10. **Guarantee** — risk reversal, stated plainly.
11. **Mechanical CTA** — tell them exactly what happens when they tap.
12. **Signature** — the brand line.
13. *(Headline, ≤40 chars — written last, after you know what the ad actually says.)*

**The order is load-bearing.** Moving the CTA earlier does not "get to the point faster"; it asks
for action from someone who hasn't been sold. Moving the mechanism before the agitation makes a
stage-3 pitch to a stage-4-aware reader (see `knowledge/schwartz.md`).

## Principles behind the structure

**1. The market doesn't care about you — sell the result of the result.**
Not "AI-powered two-way texting." Not even "more bookings." The result of the result: a hygiene
schedule that isn't leaking, without anyone on staff picking up a phone.

**2. Agitation before solution, always.**
The reader must feel the cost of doing nothing before the mechanism can land. Agitation isn't
cruelty; it's making an already-present pain articulate. Our best agitation is *specific and
mundane*: the call list nobody finishes, the hygienist checking her phone at 2pm, the columns with
holes in them.

**3. Give enormous value before asking for anything.**
The Suby model gives away the *what* and sells the *how done for you*. In our funnel: the case
study, the conversation transcripts, the actual SMS threads. Ads that teach outperform ads that
tease, because teaching is proof of competence in a market that's been burned.

**4. Every asset has one job, and it's to earn the next step.**
The ad's job is the click. The landing page's job is the application. The application's job is the
call. Each asset that tries to do the next one's job does its own worse. **The ad never sells the
sprint. The ad sells the click.**

**5. Risk reversal is not optional in a distrusted category.**
A stranger will not hand a vendor their patient list on the strength of copy. The guarantee is what
makes responding feel free.

**6. Specificity in the CTA.**
"Learn More" as the button, but the copy above it says mechanically what happens: what they'll see,
how long it takes, what you'll ask for. Ambiguity at the CTA is where earned attention leaks out.

## The hyper-dopamine ad — the creative-side framework

The 13 beats govern the body. This governs whether anyone reads the body at all.

**The two jobs of any ad:** (1) deliver **news, value, or findings** — that's what people actually
consume on Meta; (2) **sell the click.** Not the product. Not the service. The click.

**The intersection that matters:**

```
pure clickbait  ─────────  HYPER-DOPAMINE AD  ─────────  pure targeted benefit
(blind intrigue,           (burning intrigue +           (no curiosity,
 attracts everyone,         a big specific benefit        nothing to click for)
 tells the algorithm        for a specific person)
 nothing)
```

Blind intrigue attracts everybody, which means the algorithm learns nothing about who to find.
A benefit with no curiosity gives nobody a reason to act. You need both.

**The three-criteria test — run it on every ad before it ships:**
1. Does the creative **interrupt the pattern**? Is it weird enough to stop a thumb?
2. Is there **burning intrigue** in the creative *and* the headline?
3. Is a **big specific benefit** promised if they click?

**Anatomy, in the order the eye actually moves:**
1. **Scroll-stopping image** — seen first, before any words
2. **Eyeball-grabbing headline**
3. **Slippery lead-in copy** — the most overlooked, disproportionately load-bearing line in the ad
4. **Intriguing link description** — also routinely wasted
5. **Body copy** — our 13 beats
6. **Non-threatening CTA** — **"Learn More" wins split tests.** Never "Buy Now" / "Inquire Now."
   (Independently confirms our locked CTA rule.)

**The primary + secondary image device:** viral news posts pair a main image with a small inset.
The brain stalls trying to comprehend two things at once, and that stall is long enough for the
headline to get read. Cheap, transferable, underused by us.

**Creative styles that transfer to us:** *raw native* (phone-shot, unpolished, multiple people in
frame) and *native highlight* (a real photo with a red circle + arrow) are the two to use.
*Breaking news* format works everywhere but is a **trust cost in our market** — one test ad at most,
never the portfolio. Gossip/tabloid register is refused; see the analysis doc for why.

## Copy craft rules (confirmed by split testing at scale)

- **5th grade or below**, checked with the Hemingway app — target grade 3–4. "Consumption precedes
  conversion." Don't let the reader notice the writing.
- **Specificity is believability, not just proof.** "12 to 19 kilos in 5 weeks" beats "lose weight";
  "90 to 480 leads a month" beats "explode your leads." The more general the claim, the more doubt
  it generates. This is the mechanical argument for our whole proof stack.
- **Line-by-line breaks.** Split-tested and won. A wall of text reads as work.
- **Write to one person.** "You," not "our customers." "We/us," not the company name.
- **Long copy beats short — when it's interesting.** Write it, ignore length, then edit hard.
  Long copy also feeds the algorithm more context about who to target (see `meta-platform.md`).
- **Theme everything to the winning concept.** Once a headline wins, align the creative, the
  lead-in, and the body to that one angle. Commit fully; don't hedge across angles inside one ad.
- **Positive beats negative ~8/10 times** — a big-benefit promise out-pulls pain-avoidance framing
  even in painful markets. ⚠️ **Live tension with our loss-aversion positioning.** The reconciliation:
  loss *recovery* ("get back what's yours") is a benefit frame; loss *avoidance* ("don't lose another
  year") is the negative frame. Our copy currently mixes both. Flag it, test it, don't rewrite on
  assumption.
- **Above all of it:** proof, credibility, scarcity, and sentence craft all pale next to hitting the
  bullseye of what the market actually wants. Every ad is a hypothesis about desire; performance is
  the only thing that confirms it.

## Our locked constraints, in this lens's terms

- 230–250 words. Shorter loses beats; longer loses the reader.
- Headline ≤ 40 characters (current range 26–32).
- CTA: Apply Now / Learn More / Sign Up only.
- 5th-grade reading level throughout.
- The three ✅ bullets are led by the production figure — the strongest proof gets the first bullet.
- Never a cost figure adjacent to a result.

## What this lens catches

- CTA arriving before agitation and mechanism have landed
- Missing anti-disqualification (the reader opts out on an objection you never addressed)
- Mechanism stated as a feature instead of an explanation of past failure
- Word count drift — under 230 usually means a beat was silently dropped
- An ad trying to close the sprint instead of earning the click
- Guarantee mentioned but not *stated plainly enough to be believed*
- An ad that passes the 13 beats but fails the three-criteria test — technically complete, no
  reason to stop scrolling
- Generic claims where a specific number was available
- A winning headline that the creative and lead-in don't theme to
