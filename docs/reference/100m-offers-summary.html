Ch. 2What Is a Grand Slam Offer?

The offer is the starting point of every transaction — what you provide in exchange for money. Most businesses have a commodity offer (compared on price) or no real offer at all. A Grand Slam Offer creates a category of one.

Grand Slam Offer — Definition

An offer you present to the marketplace that cannot be compared to any other product or service available — combining an attractive promotion, an unmatchable value proposition, a premium price, and an unbeatable guarantee with a money model that allows you to get paid to acquire new customers.

The Offer Spectrum

Offer QualityBusiness Result
No offerNo business, no life
Bad offerNegative profit, miserable life
Decent offerNo profit, stagnating business
Good offerSome profit, okay business
Grand Slam OfferFantastic profit, insane business, freedom

Three Effects of a Grand Slam Offer

  1. Increased response rates — more people click or act on your ads
  2. Higher conversion — more prospects become buyers
  3. Premium prices — you charge more for the same fulfillment

The Two Core Problems It Solves

Problem 1

Not enough clients. Acquiring them costs too much relative to the margin you make.

Problem 2

Not enough cash. Competing on price destroys margins and races everyone to the bottom.

Pricing — How to Charge Lots of Money

Ch. 3The Commodity Problem

A commodity is available from many places and therefore purchased on price. Commoditization drives the market toward efficiency — the point where margins are just enough to keep the lights on. A Grand Slam Offer breaks you out of this entirely.

Real-World Math: Commodity vs. Grand Slam

Agency lead generation example (rounded for illustration; based on real client data):

❌ Old Commoditized Way
  • $1K down + $1K/month retainer
  • 0.5–1× ROAS: lose money on acquisition
  • Break even ~60 days after client starts
  • Identical to every other agency — price war
✓ New Grand Slam Way
  • One-time fee, no recurring charge
  • Cover ad spend only; pay on results
  • Guarantee: 20 leads in 30 days or next month free
  • Plus daily coaching, scripts, best-practice playbook
2.5× more responses × 2.5× higher close rate × 4× higher price
= 22.4× more cash collected upfront

The fulfillment is identical. The difference is how the offer is framed and what it promises. One forces price comparison; the other makes comparison impossible.

Ch. 4Finding the Right Market — A Starving Crowd

"A starving crowd. You could have the worst hot dogs, terrible prices, and be in a terrible location — but if you're the only hot dog stand when the college football game breaks out, you're going to sell out."

Four Criteria for a Good Market

  1. Massive pain — degree of pain is proportional to the price you can charge. "The pain is the pitch." If you can articulate a prospect's pain accurately, they will almost always buy.
  2. Purchasing power — your target must be able to afford what you charge. Unemployed people with resume problems are easy to find but can't pay.
  3. Easy to target — associations, mailing lists, Facebook groups, channels, subreddits. If you can't find them, a great offer falls on deaf ears regardless.
  4. Growing — growing markets are tailwinds; declining markets are headwinds. Lloyd sold great software to newspapers — a market shrinking 25%/year. Everything failed despite product quality.

The Three Timeless Markets

There will always be pain when you lack these — so demand always exists:

Health

Weight loss, pain relief, longevity, fitness, beauty

Wealth

Business growth, investing, income, debt elimination

Relationships

Dating, marriage, family, social status, networking

Order of Importance

Market > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills

A great market overcomes a bad offer and bad salesmanship. A great offer in a normal market makes you tons of money even if you're a mediocre salesperson. A great persuader in a normal market with a normal offer can succeed — but it's the hardest path. This book is about maximizing offer strength so you don't need to be exceptional at persuasion.

Commit to the Niche

Hormozi calls niche-hopping "deserving a niche slap." Every market has unpleasant characteristics. Switching niches means starting over. Pick, then commit. Most entrepreneurs fail at an offer before they fail at a market — try 100 different offers in the same niche before concluding the market is bad.

Ch. 5Charge What It's Worth

"Charge as high a price as you can say out loud without cracking a smile."

The Virtuous Cycle of Price

↓ Lower Prices
  • Lower client emotional investment
  • Lower perceived value of service
  • Worse client results (not invested)
  • Attracts worst, cheapest clients
  • Destroys margin to improve
  • Business withers
↑ Higher Prices
  • Higher client emotional investment
  • Higher perceived value
  • Better results (more adherent)
  • Attracts ideal, easiest clients
  • More margin to reinvest
  • Business compounds

The wine study: Researchers gave subjects three wines — same wine, three price tags. Participants consistently rated the "expensive" wine best. Higher price literally increased the perceived (and neurologically measured) pleasure. Raising your price changes the value you deliver without changing the product.

Why Charging Premium Is a Moral Position

  • Low-price businesses can't hire great people, invest in systems, or improve — they fail their clients
  • High-price businesses have margin to deliver exceptional results
  • Clients pay more attention and follow through more when they've paid more — better outcomes are the direct result

Gym Lords Data Point

Hormozi charged $42,000/year — more than the average gym owner's entire take-home profit of $35K. Survey of 158 gyms showed that after 11 months, average improvements were: +$239K topline revenue, +$161K recurring revenue, profit tripled from $2,943 → $8,940/month. The conviction to charge that much came from outworking his self-doubt until he knew it worked.

"99% of businesses need to raise their prices. Profit is oxygen. It fuels the fire of growth."

Section 3

Value — Create Your Offer

Ch. 6The Value Equation

The Value Equation

Value = Dream Outcome  ×  Perceived Likelihood of Achievement Perceived Time Delay  ×  Effort & Sacrifice

Increase the numerator. Drive the denominator toward zero. If the denominator reaches zero, value approaches infinity.

The Four Drivers — In Detail

1. Dream Outcome Increase

The experience and feeling the prospect envisions in their ideal future. People ultimately buy to increase their status — socially or professionally. The vehicle matters less than the destination.

Pro tip: Frame benefits in terms of status gained from the viewpoint of others. "Your golf buddy's jaw will drop when your drive sails 40 yards past theirs."

2. Perceived Likelihood of Achievement Increase

How confident is the prospect that they specifically will get the result? Would you pay more to be a surgeon's 10,000th patient or their first? People pay for certainty. Build this through track record, testimonials, guarantees, and clear proof.

3. Time Delay Decrease

The gap between purchase and receiving value. Two components:

  • Short-term experiences: early wins that confirm the decision — a first sale closed, 2 lbs lost, first lead generated. Get clients a "big emotional win" within 7 days. Backed by science: early victories predict long-term completion.
  • Long-term outcome: the dream. The faster you can deliver it, the more valuable you are.

Fast beats free. FedEx, Uber, Spotify Premium — all won in spaces where "free" already existed by being faster. If competing against free: double down on speed.

4. Effort & Sacrifice Decrease

Every ancillary cost along the way — time, discomfort, embarrassment, inconvenience. Compare fitness (5-10 hrs/week, hunger, soreness, meal prep, injury risk) vs. liposuction (fall asleep, wake up thin, 2 weeks of soreness). Identical dream outcome; orders-of-magnitude different prices.

Done-for-you services are almost always priced higher than DIY — because the effort drops to near zero.

Logical vs. Psychological Solutions

Logical solutions have usually been tried — they're logical, so everyone tries them. What remains are psychological gaps. Example:

LogicalPsychologicalCost Difference
Make trains fasterAdd dotted arrival map (passengers know when train comes)Billions vs. millions
Speed up elevatorAdd floor-to-ceiling mirrors (people forget they're waiting)10× less
Make it cheaperMake fewer, raise price — creates desire through exclusivityHigher margins

Case Study: Meditation vs. Xanax

Both deliver the same dream outcome (relaxation, decreased anxiety). But on every other driver:

DriverMeditationXanax
Dream outcome✓ (1/1)✓ (1/1)
Perceived likelihoodLow — most don't believe they'll stick with it (0/1)High — pill works (1/1)
Time delayWeeks to months (0.5/1)15 minutes (1/1)
Effort & sacrificeDiscomfort, boredom, daily time commitment (0/1)Swallow a pill (1/1)
Total value1.5/44/4

That's why Xanax is a multi-billion dollar industry and there is almost no multi-billion dollar meditation business. Value ≠ what is good for you. Value = what is perceived as valuable.

Ch. 9Creating the Grand Slam Offer — Problems & Solutions

Before building the offer, recognize that convergent thinking (one right answer) is what school trains you for — but offer creation requires divergent thinking (many possible answers to one problem). Generate possibilities first; edit later.

The Five-Step Process

  1. Identify the Dream Outcome

    Stop selling the product; sell the destination. "Lose 20 lbs in 6 weeks" not "gym membership." You're selling the vacation, not the plane flight. Make it specific and time-bounded.

  2. List Every Problem

    Write down every obstacle a prospect faces before, during, and after using your product. Think in the sequence they'll experience them. For each core step they must take, list every reason they might fail using the four value drivers as your guide (dream outcome, likelihood, effort, time). You should end up with 32–64+ problems. More problems = more opportunities to provide value.

  3. Turn Problems into Solutions

    For each problem, write a solution statement: "How to [reverse the obstacle] so that [specific avatar] can [outcome]." This is the checklist of everything your offer must address. You must solve every problem — even one unanswered objection can kill a sale.

  4. Create Delivery Vehicles (The How)

    Think of every possible way to deliver each solution. The more creative the better — you won't use everything, but ideation unlocks ideas you'd miss otherwise.

  5. Trim & Stack

    Remove high-cost/low-value items. Keep high-value/low-cost items. Then bundle everything into a final deliverable. (See next chapter.)

Dimensions to Consider for Each Solution

The 10× / ÷10 Test

If your customer paid 10× your price — what would you provide? If they paid 1/10th the price and you still had to make them successful, how would you do it cheaper/faster? Stretching in both directions unlocks delivery options you'd never think of at the status quo.

Key insight: When Hormozi needed a sale to pay rent and a prospect said "I eat out every day," he improvised an eating-out guide on the spot. From that point on, he never lost that objection again. Never get romantic about how you want to solve the problem — find a way to solve every problem the prospect raises.

Ch. 10Trim and Stack — Building the Final Deliverable

The Sales-to-Fulfillment Continuum

Every business sits on a spectrum:

You do moreYou do less
Easy to sellHard to sell
Hard to fulfill (time-intensive)Easy to fulfill

Hormozi's rule: "Create flow, monetize flow, then add friction." Get demand moving first. Add friction (efficiency, reduced fulfillment) only after you know what clients need and cash is coming in. Don't optimize a business with zero customers.

What to Keep — What to Cut

  • Keep: High value / Low cost items and High value / High cost items (when justified)
  • Cut: High cost / Low value items first, then Low cost / Low value items
  • Focus especially on 1-to-many solutions — high upfront cost to create, near-zero marginal cost to deliver

Section 4

Enhancing Your Offer

Ch. 11The Delicate Dance of Desire

All marketing artificially influences the supply-demand curve. To maximize profit: maximize demand, minimize perceived supply. The five enhancement tools — scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, and naming — do exactly this.

"Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want." — Naval Ravikant

Desire only exists for things you don't have. The moment desire is satisfied, it disappears. Therefore: sell fewer units than you can to keep desire elevated in your market.

Hormozi's Law

The longer you delay the ask, the bigger the ask you can make. The longer the runway, the bigger the plane that can take off. Keep supply and satisfaction of desire slightly below the demand you generate. This maximizes profits and keeps desire ravenous.

Fractal 80/20: Roughly 1/5 of your prospects are willing to pay 5× your asking price. If you sell 10 spots at $500, 2 of those people would have paid $5,000. A limited-supply scenario lets you skim the top and leave the others with increased desire — which makes the next launch even stronger.

Ch. 12Scarcity — Sold Out

Scarcity limits quantity. The fear of missing out is a more powerful motivator than the desire for gain. Humans hoard scarce resources.

Three Types of Scarcity

  1. Limited supply of seats/slots (total or per period)
  2. Limited supply of bonuses
  3. Never available again

Service Business Scarcity Tactics

Tactic 1

Total Business Cap

Only accept X clients total. Create a waitlist. When demand increases, bump cap by 10-20%, then cap again. Works especially well for high-ticket tiers.

Tactic 2

Growth Rate Cap

"We only accept 5 new clients per week. First 3 spots are taken." Tell prospects exactly how many openings remain — they'll act accordingly.

Tactic 3

Cohort Cap

Only X clients per class/cohort. Start clients monthly or quarterly. Creates operational choreography and legitimate sales urgency simultaneously.

Honest Scarcity

The simplest strategy: be truthful about your capacity. You can't handle 1,000 clients tomorrow — how many can you take this week? Advertise that number. "We're 81% to capacity" creates urgency and implies social proof simultaneously. You define where "full" is.

Always Sell Out

Better to sell out consistently than to over-order and destroy the scarcity narrative. Announce your sellouts — people on the fence see that others thought it was worth it, and their desire intensifies for next time. Chanel ships 1–2 pieces per store per style for exactly this reason.

Ch. 13Urgency — Deadlines Drive Decisions

Scarcity limits quantity. Urgency limits time. A defined deadline or cutoff forces action from people who would otherwise defer indefinitely.

Four Ethical Urgency Methods

Method 1

Rolling Cohorts

"Our next group kicks off Monday. Sign up now or wait for the next start date." The less frequently you start cohorts, the more powerful this is.

Method 2

Rolling Seasonal

New Year promo → Valentine's promo → Spring promo. Same core offer; new name and date each cycle. "This promotion ends January 30th." Deadlines are real; just the wrapper changes.

Method 3

Pricing/Bonus Urgency

"I'm not sure how long we're running this promotion — we change them every 4 weeks." Creates FOMO around the deal, not the service. Maintains integrity.

Method 4

Exploding Opportunity

An arbitrage opportunity that decays with time (crypto windows, ad platform arbitrage, job offers). Every day they wait, the opportunity shrinks. True urgency, not manufactured.

50–60% of Sales in the Last 4 Hours

In a week-long launch, the final 3% of the time period generates 50–60% of sales. Most people who seemed interested but didn't buy would never have bought without the deadline. The deadline converts the fence-sitters; the people who "miss out" were never going to buy anyway.

Ch. 14Bonuses — It's All Gravy, Baby

"A single offer is less valuable than the same offer broken into its component parts and stacked as bonuses."

This is how infomercials work. They price one knife at $38.95 — then stack 37 more knives, sharpeners, pans, and guarantees until the price-to-value gap is so wide the prospect snaps. We anchor to the core price, then expand perceived value with each bonus until it's irrational not to buy.

Presenting Bonuses in 1:1 Sales

  • Ask for the sale first. If they say yes → reveal bonuses as a "wow" reinforcement of their decision
  • If they say no → present a bonus that directly addresses their specific objection, then ask again
  • Repeat. People struggle to refuse reciprocity

10 Rules for Powerful Bonuses

  1. Always offer them — present the entire bundle
  2. Give each a special name that contains a benefit ("Foolproof Bargain Grocery System," not "bonus 3")
  3. Explain: what it is, how you discovered it, how it improves their life
  4. Provide proof — statistic, past client, personal result
  5. Paint a vivid mental image of life after using it
  6. Always assign a dollar value and justify it
  7. Tools and checklists > additional training (lower effort = higher perceived value)
  8. Each should address a specific obstacle the prospect believes will stop them
  9. Solve their next problem before they encounter it
  10. The total value of bonuses should eclipse the core offer value — this subconsciously elevates the core's perceived worth

Advanced: Other People's Products (Free Bonuses)

Approach adjacent businesses — chiropractors, supplement companies, pharmacists, health clubs. They give you their product/service as a bonus in exchange for exposure to your qualified clients. You get high-value bonuses at zero cost; they get warm referrals.

Pain Clinic Example

  • Massage therapist: 1–2 free massages ($100+ value)
  • Chiropractor: 2 free adjustments ($100 value)
  • Low-inflammation food brand: product discount ($50 savings)
  • Local gym: free personal training session + 1 month membership ($100 value)
  • Pharmacy: drug discount cards ($100/month in savings)

If the core offer is $400, these bonuses alone justify the price. Then negotiate affiliate commissions — the business you "gave away" referrals to now pays you per conversion, making each bonus a revenue stream.

Add Bonuses, Never Discount

Discounting teaches customers your prices are negotiable and signals weakness. Adding bonuses to close a deal signals strength and goodwill — and it protects your core offer price forever.

Ch. 15Guarantees — Reversing Risk

The #1 objection for any product or service is risk — risk that it won't work for them. Reversing risk is the fastest way to increase conversions. Hormozi saw guarantees double-to-quadruple conversion in some markets.

The Math on Guarantees

Don't Be Emotional — Do the Math

If a guarantee increases closes by 30% but doubles your refund rate (5% → 10%):
Without guarantee: 100 sales − 5 refunds = 95 net sales
With guarantee: 130 sales − 13 refunds = 117 net sales
117 ÷ 95 = 1.23× — a 23% increase in net revenue.
For refunds to offset the gain, every additional sale would need to refund — mathematically almost impossible.

Four Types of Guarantees

TypeHow It WorksBest For
UnconditionalNo questions asked refund. Full trial period. Highest conversion, highest risk.Low-ticket B2C
ConditionalRefund/service/credit contingent on doing specific things. Ties guarantee to success behaviors.Most businesses
Anti-guaranteeAll sales final. Position with a compelling "reason why" — usually IP/knowledge exposure.Consumable/IP products
ImpliedPerformance/rev-share model. If you don't perform, you don't get paid. Perfect alignment.Quantifiable outcomes

Conditional Guarantee Examples — 10 Types

Conditional

Outsized Refund

2–3× money back if conditions not met. Jason Fladley: "If you follow the method and don't profit, I'll buy your store for $25K." Result: $3M in extra sales; only 10 payouts.

Conditional

Service Guarantee

Keep working for free until they hit the result. Eliminates time pressure on the guarantee. Hormozi: no one has ever actually invoked it once they're close to their goal.

Conditional

Delayed 2nd Payment

"We won't bill you again until you make your first sale." Focuses both sides on the first win — the activation event that predicts long-term retention.

Conditional

First Outcome

"If you don't make your first sale in 14 days, we'll cover your ad spend until you do." Keeps everyone focused on the first dollar.

Conditional

Credit Guarantee

Refund returns as credit toward any service. Best for upsells — worst case, they apply it to what they already love.

Conditional

Personal Service

"Alex will personally work with you until your offer converts." Add conditions (must spend $X on testing, etc.) to make it meaningful but achievable.

Stacking Guarantees

Layer multiple types together: an unconditional 30-day money-back + a conditional 90-day triple-your-money-back if you complete the program. The stack future-paces the prospect into believing the result is achievable and shows you're serious about it.

Name Your Guarantee

Instead of "30-day satisfaction guarantee," try: "The Club a Baby Seal Guarantee — if after 30 days you wouldn't club a baby seal to stay on as a customer, you don't owe a penny." Memorable names make guarantees more credible and more talked about.

Ch. 16Naming — The MAGIC Formula

A grand slam offer that no one hears about makes no money. The name determines response rate. A great name on the same offer can get 2–10× the responses. And when offers fatigue in local markets, renaming the wrapper — not rebuilding the offer — is almost always the fix.

The MAGIC Formula

Use 3–5 components. Shorter and punchier generally outperforms. Test multiple names; keep the winner.

M

Magnetic Reason Why

Why this offer, why now? "Free," "88% off," "Grand Opening," "New Management," seasonal hook. Answers: why should I respond?

A

Announce Your Avatar

Call out exactly who this is for. Hyper-local beats generic: not "Baltimore moms" but "Towson moms." The more specific, the higher the response from the right people.

G

Give Them a Goal

The dream outcome in one phrase: "celebrity smile," "pain-free," "double your profit," "first client," "little black dress." Specific and tangible beats vague and inspirational.

I

Indicate an Interval

Duration of the program or time to result: "21-day," "6-week," "12-month." Note: platforms restrict quantified outcome + duration claims (implies guarantee). Use where compliant.

C

Container Word

Signals this is a bundled system, not a single service: Challenge, Blueprint, Intensive, Masterclass, Boot Camp, Sprint, Accelerator, Transformation, Shortcut, Deep Dive, Liftoff…

When Offers Fatigue — What to Change (In Order)

  1. Change the creative (images, video)
  2. Change the body copy in ads
  3. Change the headline / name of the offer
  4. Change the seasonality (New Year → Valentine's → Spring)
  5. Change the duration (6 weeks → 28 days → 8 weeks)
  6. Change the free/discount component
  7. Change the entire monetization structure — last resort only

The actual value stack rarely changes. Only the wrapper does.
